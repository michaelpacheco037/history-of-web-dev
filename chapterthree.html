03 Chapter 3 / The 2000s

The rise of Web 2.0

Profiles, shared knowledge, and applications bring people into the picture.

The early 2000s

Visitors become participants

Websites increasingly invited people to create profiles, post updates, and connect with others. Services such as Friendster, MySpace, and Facebook helped make social interaction a familiar part of being online.

The phrase “Web 2.0” described this shift toward participation. It was not a new version of HTML or a single software upgrade; it was a way of talking about a more interactive web.

The mid-to-late 2000s

Websites become useful applications

Maps, video platforms, online documents, and file-sharing services made the browser a place to complete everyday tasks. Instead of only reading information, visitors could create, edit, and collaborate.

JavaScript and techniques such as Ajax allowed parts of a page to update without reloading the entire document. The experience began to feel more like using an application.

Throughout the 2000s

Content comes from the community

Wikis, blogs, reviews, and discussion forums let users contribute what they knew. A website’s value could come from the people using it as much as from its original publisher.

Building for these communities also meant thinking about accounts, moderation, privacy, and how to make a growing amount of information easy to find.

A change in the visitor’s role

The early web helped people find documents. The participatory web also gave them tools to make and share their own.
